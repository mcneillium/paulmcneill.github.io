Reproducible studies on public data

Four studies. One is still running and one is a first look. Each has a protocol fixed before any model was fitted, baselines before anything complex, fixed and fingerprinted splits, tests, and a log of every decision, including the ones that turned out wrong.

All four ask the same thing from different sides: what can a model be trusted to say when its data is incomplete, its labels are noisy, or the place it is used differs from the place it was trained?

River early warning across 671 British catchments

{% include listen.html key="river" %}

{% comment %} TODO: REPLACE (Paul): the research question in one sentence, and what you expected to find, dated. {% endcomment %}

Setup

  • The question behind the design: how far ahead can river flow and flood or low-flow warnings be forecast without knowing future weather, and does that skill carry over to places the model was not trained on?
  • Daily river flow and weather for 671 catchments, 1970 to 2022. 659 had enough data to use.
  • Task: on day t, forecast flow 1, 3 and 7 days ahead, using only what is known on day t. No future rainfall.
  • Train on 1970 to 2004, choose settings on 2004 to 2012, test on 2012 to 2022.
  • Warnings: flow above a catchment's 95th percentile (high) or below its 10th (low), with thresholds taken from the training years.

  • Most of the skill beyond a day depends on knowing the weather. At 7 days the model reaches 0.24 without future weather and 0.72 with it. Median NSE at 7 days is 0.242 for per-catchment gradient boosting with no weather after the day the forecast is made, and 0.722 for the same model given the observed weather over the forecast window. The second figure is an upper bound, not a forecast.
  • A whole unseen region costs little. One regional model, tested on regions held out of training, loses a median of 0.019 NSE at 1 day. Five geographic folds, each region forecast by a regional model trained on the other four. Median NSE at 1 day falls from 0.762 to 0.723. The median loss per catchment is 0.019.
  • A neural network did no better. An LSTM reading the last 365 days of raw data scored 0.73, 0.38 and 0.24 at 1, 3 and 7 days, against 0.76, 0.38 and 0.24 for boosting, and no better on regions it had never seen. That points to the missing weather forecast as the limit, not the choice of model. Seven alternative settings, chosen on the validation years only, did no better. A regional LSTM (hidden size 256, 365-day window, three seeds, one fixed configuration set before the run) has median NSE 0.734, 0.377 and 0.239 against 0.764, 0.375 and 0.235 for regional boosting on identical days. The median difference per catchment is -0.019, +0.002 and +0.002. Under the rule fixed in the protocol (within 0.01 counts as no better) it is a null result at every lead. On regions held out of training it scores 0.708, 0.367 and 0.225 against 0.723, 0.367 and 0.224, so the null result holds there too. A follow-up tried seven alternative settings (learning rate, size, weight decay, finer early stopping and a 90-day window), each chosen on the 2005 to 2012 validation years only: none beat the original, and the 90-day window cost about 0.02 NSE at 1 day and nothing at 3 or 7 days.
  • A different climate costs much more. Trained on wet catchments and tested on dry ones, NSE falls from 0.850 to 0.752. A random-half control loses about 0.03. A regional model trained on the wetter half of the catchments and tested on the drier half scores NSE 0.752, against 0.850 for the model trained on all of them. A random-half control loses about 0.03. Low-flow CSI falls from 0.659 to 0.414. Each figure is the mean of three seeds.
  • The neural network suffers more from a different climate. Trained only on the wetter catchments, the LSTM lost about twice as much skill on the drier ones as boosting at 1 day, and many times more at 3 and 7 days. In most of the dry catchments its low-flow warnings never scored a hit. Trained on a random half instead, it transferred well, so the climate is the problem, not the smaller training set. Cost of transfer per catchment: NSE of the model trained on fewer catchments minus the model of the same family trained on all of them, median over the 167 dry catchments outside the control half and three seeds. Wet-trained: LSTM -0.106, -0.076 and -0.062 at 1, 3 and 7 days, against -0.050, -0.007 and -0.014 for boosting; the per-seed ranges do not overlap. Random half: LSTM -0.004, +0.001 and 0.000, against -0.015, -0.002 and -0.002. The wet-trained LSTM's low-flow CSI is zero in 75 to 88% of catchment-seed pairs, against 24 to 57% for wet-trained boosting. The measure and its 0.01 margin were fixed before the run; the follow-up itself was planned after the first neural-model results.
  • A single-number forecast fails as a warning system at 7 days (high-flow critical success index 0.005). Warnings taken from forecast quantiles reach 0.175. A model-free band around today's flow reaches 0.155, so the model's edge is narrow. At 7 days the single-number regional model has a high-flow CSI of 0.005, against 0.138 for persistence. Warning when a forecast quantile crosses the threshold gives 0.175, and a persistence band gives 0.155. The quantile was chosen on 2009 to 2012 only.
  • Most multi-day high-flow warnings are false alarms. For the quantile model 75 to 80% are wrong. For the two persistence baselines the share runs from 64% to 82%. High-flow false alarm ratio at 3 and 7 days: 0.753 and 0.802 for the quantile model, and between 0.635 and 0.816 for persistence and the persistence band.
  • The model's 90% range is honest where it was trained (coverage 0.90 to 0.91 after conformal calibration) and less so elsewhere: on unseen regions about one catchment in six gets a range that covers under 80%. Winter is the weak season: coverage dips below target on seen catchments, and on unseen regions about one catchment in four falls under 80%. After conformalized quantile regression on 2009 to 2012, the 90% interval covers 0.90 to 0.91 of days on seen catchments at every lead. On held-out regions the median catchment is at 0.89 to 0.90, but 14 to 18% of catchments fall under 0.80. Repeated with three model seeds, every one of these findings held. By season of the target date, conformal coverage on seen catchments is 0.884 to 0.892 in December to February, against 0.905 to 0.917 in the other seasons; on unseen regions 22 to 26% of catchments fall under 0.80 in winter, against 9 to 22% otherwise. The calibration was not changed; this is a breakdown of the same ranges.
  • A general-purpose forecasting model, used without training, is a strong baseline but no substitute. Chronos-2 beat "same as today" at every lead but stayed well below the trained models, and giving it past weather made no difference. In two catchments checked, the river and the LSTM rose after the wettest days while Chronos-2 forecast a fall. Chronos-2 (amazon/chronos-2), 365 days of history, no training or tuning on these rivers. Median paired NSE difference against the seen LSTM, per catchment on identical days and averaged over the LSTM's three seeds: -0.129, -0.073 and -0.083 at 1, 3 and 7 days (Chronos-2 better in 15%, 7% and 5% of catchments); against persistence +0.062, +0.234 and +0.374. With past rain, temperature and evaporation as inputs: -0.0004, -0.00003 and -0.0008 against flow only. Four of seven predictions recorded before the run were met; the 1-day gap was much larger than predicted. The rain-response check covers two catchments, so it is an illustration, not a finding.

What went wrong, and what changed

  • The first diagnosis of a weak baseline (early stopping) was wrong. A controlled comparison on validation years showed the target scale was the cause. The log records both.
  • Twelve of 51 quantile models hit the tree limit in the first run (9, 7 and 6 in the three-seed repeat), so those are under-trained. Reported, not hidden.
  • A follow-up run overwrote the main neural-model tables with a shorter version. They were restored from the record and the script was fixed so it cannot happen again.
  • Repeating the uncertainty experiment on a second operating system exposed a rounding tie in the persistence-band baseline: flows recorded exactly at the low-flow threshold can switch between warning and no warning. The published band results are unchanged and the effect is logged.

Limits

  • "Unseen" is not "ungauged": recent flow at the catchment is still an input.
  • Coverage during floods specifically has not been checked.
  • The neural model's settings were checked against seven alternatives on validation years only; the search was small, and a broader one could do better.
  • One foundation model was tested, given raw flow and a 365-day history.
  • Still to do: gaps in the inputs, and explanations.

Code, protocols and decision log: github.com/mcneillium/river-early-warning

Setup

  • 924 cancer cell lines. One gene, PTEN, is a known but incomplete guide to how they respond to drugs that block the AKT protein.
  • The question behind the design: how much more of the response does wider molecular data explain, and can the patterns be trusted?
  • 5-fold cross-validation repeated 5 times on saved folds. Every step that learns from data happens inside the training folds.
  • Cell lines are a stand-in. Nothing here is about patients or treatment.

Results

  • Mostly a negative result. Wider data helps a little. More than four fifths of the response stays unexplained. Held-out R² rises from 0.112 (PTEN, cancer type and sequencing centre) to 0.144 with an elastic net on 2,000 genes and 0.187 with gradient boosting. The gains are +0.033 (95% interval +0.005 to +0.060) and +0.076 (+0.014 to +0.130). The difference between the two models, +0.043 (-0.003 to +0.086), is not clear of zero.
  • That gap is not mainly measurement noise. Where a related drug was measured twice, about 80% of the variation repeats, and the best model explains 18%. Exploratory. A related drug, Uprosertib, was measured twice at the same doses and the two measurements agree at 0.81 (0.76 to 0.85). The best model for that drug reaches R² 0.18. The ceiling is from the related drug, not from Ipatasertib.
  • A common mistake produces a believable result from noise. The false score is as large as the real one. Choosing genes by correlation with response on all cell lines, then cross-validating, gives R² 0.210 on the real response and 0.137 on a shuffled one. Choosing inside each fold gives -0.005 and -0.173.
  • No stable subgroups beyond tissue of origin were found. Made-up data with no groups at all scores 0.80 to 0.87 on the usual stability measure when cut in two, so stability alone proves little. Among the 128 PTEN-altered cell lines, three clusters score stability 1.00 against 0.68 for reference data with no groups, and 94% of cell lines sit in the commonest cluster of their own tissue. After removing tissue and sequencing centre, no clustering is both stable and clearly above the reference.
  • Explanations change when the model is refitted. Only the original marker and one gene are reliably in the top 20. Top-20 feature lists from 25 refits (5 folds, 5 repeats) overlap by 0.20 on average (Jaccard index). Only PTEN status and one gene are in the top 20 at least 80% of the time.

What went wrong, and what changed

  • Gains were first computed on predictions averaged over repeats, which flattered the less stable model (+0.094 became +0.076). Corrected, with a test.
  • A sanity check declared in advance turned out nearly empty: 10 of the 12 pathway genes it named had been filtered out before the models saw them.
  • The pre-set bar for "stable clusters" had no reference to compare with. A no-groups reference was added before the run.
  • Three analyses were added after seeing results. They are labelled exploratory in the protocol and wherever they are reported.

Limits

  • One drug screen from one laboratory. No independent dataset.
  • Missing protein data was simulated at random. Models were not tuned.
  • No biological interpretation has been checked by a biologist.

In progress, reported part-way. Started on 17 November 2026. Every figure below comes from development data: 252 hand-labelled chips from floods the models also saw in training. The held-out evaluation, on 90 test chips and on 15 chips from a flood never used in training, has its protocol and my prediction on record and has not been run. Nothing here is a final result.

Setup

  • The question: when flood-mapping models are trained on Sentinel-1 Otsu labels, does a pretrained encoder with a small decoder repeat fewer of the label rule's errors than a UNet trained from scratch? Does updating the encoder change that?
  • Sen1Floods11: satellite radar images (Sentinel-1) of flood events, cut into square tiles called chips. 4,384 chips carry automatic labels and 446 were labelled by hand.
  • The automatic labels come from Otsu's method, a rule that calls a pixel water when its radar echo is weaker than a threshold taken from the image itself. They are cheap to make, and they are wrong in places.
  • Three models, all trained on the automatic labels only: 3,740 chips, with a further 420 held back to decide when to stop. No hand label touches training. Three random seeds each, radar only.
  • A UNet trained from scratch (7.8 million weights), and TerraMind v1 base, a model pretrained on Earth-observation data, with a small decoder: once with its encoder frozen, so that 1.7 million of its 87.1 million weights are trained, and once with the encoder updated at a low learning rate.
  • The measure: with the hand label as the reference, how much of the labels' false water ({{ flood.pixels.text.otsu_false_water }} million pixels) and missed water ({{ flood.pixels.text.otsu_missed_water }} million) does each model repeat? A lower rate is not an improvement on its own: a map that calls everything dry repeats none of the false water. So it is read beside how often the model stays right where the labels are right, and beside water IoU.
  • Before each run I recorded a prediction and the rule for any claim, and committed both before the result existed. "Repeats fewer" needs a fall of at least 5 points for false water, or 2 points for missed water, on the three-seed mean, with at least two of the three seed pairs agreeing.

  • The automatic labels are wrong in two different ways, depending on the flood. They find 69% of the hand-labelled water, and 57% of what they call water is water. Three floods hold about 80% of the false water. In six of the ten floods, missed water is the bigger error. On the 252 development chips the Otsu labels score recall 68.6%, precision 56.9% and IoU 0.451 against the hand labels, and false water is 1.65 times missed water. Pakistan, Paraguay and Somalia hold about 80% of the false water. I had predicted the reverse: recall near 50%, precision near 65% and more missed water than false water. This count drops fewer pixels than the model comparisons, which also leave out pixels with no radar data, so precision reads 56.9% here and 57.0% there.
  • A model trained from scratch copies most of those mistakes, and still edges past the labels. The UNet repeats 75% of the labels' false water and 93% of their missed water. Its water IoU is 0.474, against 0.452 for the labels it learned from. UNet, three seeds: 75.2% of Otsu's false water repeated (65.6, 78.0 and 82.0 by seed) and 92.8% of its missed water (92.5, 92.2, 93.9), while staying right on 99.18% of the pixels Otsu labels correctly. Between 86 and 90% of the UNet's own false water, and 91 to 93% of its missed water, lies on pixels where Otsu made the same mistake. Pooled water IoU is 0.4739 against 0.4516, a gain of 0.022 where the rule set in advance asked for 0.010. I predicted 75% and 80% for the two rates and an IoU of 0.47. I also predicted higher precision, which held (60.7% against 57.0%), and a clear drop in recall, which did not (68.4% against 68.6%).
  • The pretrained model did not repeat fewer of the mistakes. It repeated more. With its encoder frozen it repeats 88.9% of the false water and 94.5% of the missed water, more than the UNet in all three seed pairs. I had predicted the opposite for false water: 63%. TerraMind v1 base with the encoder frozen, its weights fingerprinted before and after every seed: 88.9% of Otsu's false water repeated (88.7, 87.0, 90.8) against the UNet's 75.2%, a mean gap of 13.7 points and 8.9 to 23.2 points by seed pair. Missed water repeated is 94.5% against 92.8%, higher in all three pairs. By the rule: no "repeats fewer" claim. Two of my six predicted directions were met. The model adds fewer mistakes of its own (186 thousand new false-water pixels against 303 thousand) but corrects far fewer of Otsu's (314 thousand against 700 thousand), so its water IoU, 0.463, sits between the labels' and the UNet's.
  • Letting the encoder learn made it a closer copy still. Updated during training, the same model repeats 95.8% of the false water and 97.2% of the missed water, more than its frozen counterpart in every seed pair. This time I predicted the direction of every change correctly, and still underestimated the size. Encoder learning rate 0.00002, decoder 0.001. Every seed starts from the published encoder and from the frozen run's starting decoder weights for that seed. Against the frozen model, paired by seed, false water repeated rises by 3.9 to 8.8 points (mean 7.0; I predicted 2.6) and missed water repeated by 1.7 to 3.3 points. By the rule: no "repeats fewer" claim. All twelve predicted directions held: six measures, against the frozen model and against the UNet. Its total false water, 2.81 million pixels, is almost the labels' own 2.82 million, and its water IoU is 0.454 against the labels' 0.452.
  • A pattern to test, not yet a finding: the closer a model matches the labels it learned from, the more of their mistakes it keeps and the fewer new ones it adds. New false water falls from 303 to 186 to 111 thousand pixels across the three models. False water put right falls faster, from 700 to 314 to 118 thousand. Agreement with the Otsu labels on the held-back stop chips (IoU) is 0.87 to 0.91 for the UNet, 0.94 for the frozen model and 0.97 for the updated one, the same order as the share of Otsu's errors repeated. The ordering was noticed after the results. It holds across the three kinds of model, which differ in several ways at once, and among the UNet's seeds, but not among the pretrained model's own seeds. It is a hypothesis for the next experiment.

What went wrong, and what changed

  • My main prediction was wrong. I expected the frozen pretrained encoder to repeat less of the labels' false water than the UNet (63% against 75.2%). It repeated 88.9%. The prediction and the result are both in the record.
  • My first prediction about the labels was wrong as well: I expected missed water to be the larger error, and false water is.
  • An early count saved totals, not the masks behind them. Equal totals can come from different pixels, so every comparison since 19 November first proves, chip by chip, that the models are scored on identical pixels. The reference for that check had to be rebuilt and tied to the earlier count by its recorded numbers.
  • One UNet seed kept an earlier checkpoint (epoch 31 of 40) and copied the least false water. That widens the gap for its seed pair to 23.2 points, against 9.0 and 8.9 for the other two. All three are reported.

Limits

  • Development chips only, from floods that are also in the training data. The 90 test chips and the 15 Bolivia chips have not been opened, and Bolivia is a single flood.
  • The UNet and the pretrained model differ in architecture, size and pretraining at once, so the gap between them cannot be put down to pretraining alone. The two pretrained runs are the closer comparison.
  • The pretrained model is scored as four tiles joined together and the UNet on whole chips, and each uses its own input scaling.
  • Three seeds, one pretrained model at one size, radar only, and one untuned training recipe.
  • The hand labels are treated as the truth.
  • It is not known whether the pretrained model's training images overlap these floods.

Protocols, predictions and the decision log are kept with the code. The repository is not public yet.

A first look, run on 23 November 2026. One simulated town, two versions of one rule, and seven repairs in the test year. The rule and my predictions were committed before the test year was opened. The code was written with AI tools, to my rule and predictions. I have since re-run it on my own machine, and the numbers matched.

Setup

  • The question: when leak repairs are inferred from a drop in night flow, how many of the real repairs does the rule find, and how many of the repairs it flags are real?
  • A model trained on repair records learns from whatever produced those records. Where a rule produced them, the rule's mistakes are in the labels, as the automatic labels' mistakes are in the flood study above.
  • L-Town, the public benchmark of the Battle of the Leakage Detection and Isolation Methods (2020): a simulated town with flow and tank level every 5 minutes for 2018 and 2019. Because it is simulated, the start and end of every leak are known. No water company's data is used.
  • Night flow is the town's inflow between 02:00 and 04:00 with tank filling taken off. It averaged {{ leak.dev_night_flow }} m³/h in 2018 and {{ leak.test_night_flow }} m³/h in 2019.
  • The rule flags a repair on a day when the nights after it average lower than the nights before it by at least a threshold. The threshold was chosen on 2018, which has 10 repairs. The rule was then run once on 2019, which has 7. A flag counts when it falls within 2 days of a real repair.
  • Two versions. I chose 3 nights each side first, and predicted for it. On 2018 the night flow turned out to have a weekly rhythm, so I added 7 nights each side as a second version, with its own prediction, before 2019 was opened.

  • The rule I chose first found 3 of the 7 repairs. That is what I predicted (2 or 3). The 7-night version found 6, more than the 4 or 5 I predicted. Thresholds set on 2018 by highest F1: 34.5 m³/h for 3 nights each side and 10.0 m³/h for 7. On 2018 they found 7 and 8 of the 10 repairs. On 2019, run once: 3 of 7 and 6 of 7. Seven repairs is a small test: one repair more or less is 14 points.
  • Which repairs the 3-night rule found did not follow the size of the leak. It found leaks of 17.9, 20.4 and 21.6 m³/h and missed three larger ones, of 26.2, 26.5 and 28.2 m³/h. In 2018 the night flow averaged {{ leak.weekday_low }} to {{ leak.weekday_quiet_high }} m³/h on five nights of the week and {{ leak.weekday_busy_low }} and {{ leak.weekday_busy_high }} m³/h on the other two. A 3-night window measures that rhythm together with any repair: at thresholds up to 3 m³/h the rule raised 52 flags in 2018, one a week. At the three repairs it found in 2019 the drop it measured (35.3 to 38.3 m³/h) was larger than the leak that was repaired. My reading, not yet tested: whether this rule finds a repair depends partly on where the repair falls in the week.
  • No flag was false. Every repair either rule flagged, in either year, was a real one, so in this town the rule's mistakes are all misses. I had predicted that only 50 to 80% of the 3-night rule's flags would be real. 3 of 3 flags real for the 3-night rule in 2019 and 6 of 6 for the 7-night rule; 7 of 7 and 8 of 8 in 2018. In this simulated town, at the thresholds chosen on 2018, no drop without a repair was flagged.
  • The 7-night rule's one miss came six days after another repair. Two repairs, on 1 and 7 February, got one flag between them. The rule allows one flag within 7 days either side, so two repairs a week apart merge. Its two misses in 2018, on 2 and 12 June, each came within 14 days of another repair as well.

What went wrong, and what changed

  • The rule I chose first reacts to a weekly rhythm in night flow that I had not allowed for. The 2018 run showed it, and the 7-night version was added by a dated amendment before 2019 was opened.
  • My prediction that 50 to 80% of the 3-night rule's flags would be real was wrong: all of them were. My prediction for the 7-night rule, 4 or 5 repairs found, was one too low.
  • One of the code's tests had a wrong expected value. The code was right, and the test was corrected before the first commit.

Limits

  • The town is simulated. No flag was false here, and that should not be expected of a real network, where changes in demand, valve operations and meter faults also produce drops.
  • Seven test repairs, one town, two versions of one rule, and one way of choosing the threshold.
  • The rule stands in for the idea of inferring a repair from a drop in flow. It is not any water company's rule.
  • The first runs were made with AI tools and recounted with separate code. I then re-ran the study on my own machine, and the numbers matched.
  • No model has been trained on these labels yet, so this measures the labels' errors and not what a model does with them.

Protocol, predictions and the decision log are kept with the code. The repository is not public yet. Data: Vrachimis and others (2020), Dataset of BattLeDIM, Zenodo, doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.4017659.

How the studies are run

  • Protocol firstThe design is written and dated before any model is fitted. Later changes are dated amendments.
  • Baselines firstEvery model is compared with the simplest thing that could work, on exactly the same held-out data.
  • Fixed splitsSaved once with a fingerprint. The code refuses to overwrite them.
  • Nothing learned from held-out dataFeature choice, scaling, gap filling and tuning all happen inside the training data.
  • Negative results reportedCriteria for "this did not work" are set in advance and reported against.
  • A log of every decisionIncluding the mistakes, how they were found and what was changed.

How this work was produced

All four studies were built making use of AI tools, under research rules I set; each study’s log records how. I re-ran the river and drug response studies on my own hardware and reviewed every logged decision. In the flood study I set the design, record a prediction before each result and run the training on my own GPU; some of its logged choices still await my review. In the leak repair study I chose the rule and recorded a prediction before each run; the code was written with AI tools, I re-ran the study on my own machine, and I have not yet reviewed its logged choices.
